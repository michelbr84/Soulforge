# Voice

Read this while drafting and again during the self-check. A `SOUL.md` fails in one
predictable way: it comes out sounding like a company wrote it. That register is easy to
produce and impossible to believe, and once a document has it, nobody quotes it, nobody
consults it, and it stops mattering within a month.

## The one test that catches almost everything

**Swap the project's name for a competitor's. Every sentence that stays true is generic.**

Origin Story, The Problem We Refuse to Accept, Principles, and What We Will Never Become
*must* break under that swap. If a whole section survives it, the section says nothing —
delete it and replace it with something from the interview that is only true here.

The corollary, applied sentence by sentence while drafting: if you could paste this line
into a different project's document unchanged, it is carrying no information.

## Phrases to cut

Not because they are ugly, but because they are interchangeable. A phrase that fits every
project describes none.

| Cut | Why | Write instead |
|---|---|---|
| revolutionize / disrupt the industry | Claims an outcome nobody can verify | What specifically changes for one user |
| cutting-edge, next-generation, state-of-the-art | Dates the document within a year | The actual technique, named |
| empowering users | Says nothing about what they can now do | The specific thing they can now do |
| innovative ecosystem, platform, solution | Category words standing in for the thing | The thing |
| seamless, frictionless, effortless | Every product claims this | What used to take steps and no longer does |
| world-class, best-in-class, industry-leading | Unfalsifiable ranking | Nothing — cut the sentence |
| game-changing, paradigm shift | Marketing intensifier | The concrete change |
| robust and scalable | True of all software people are proud of | The load it actually handles, or nothing |
| passionate about | Describes a feeling, not a fact | What that feeling made you build |
| "At its core, X is about…" | Preamble that delays the sentence | Start with the sentence |
| "We're not just an X, we're a Y." | A structure, not a claim | Say what you are, once |
| "in today's fast-paced world" | Filler | Delete |
| leverage *(as a verb)*, utilize | Longer synonyms for use | use — the noun "leverage", meaning who holds power, is fine |
| best practices | Appeals to an authority that isn't named | The practice, and who it is good for |

The scan target is zero. If one survives, it needs a reason you can say out loud.

If the interview happened in another language, the same trap exists there and translates
in — Portuguese `revolucionar`, `solução inovadora`, `tecnologia de ponta`, `empoderar`,
`ecossistema`, `disruptivo`, `referência no mercado`; Spanish `de vanguardia`,
`solución integral`. Translate the person's concrete nouns, not their adjectives.

## What to write instead

**One fact beats three adjectives.** "Fast, reliable, and efficient" tells a reader
nothing. "It parses a 40MB schema in under a second, and that number is in CI so it stays
true" tells them what kind of people made this.

**Name real things.** Tools, years, decisions, numbers, the specific competitor that
disappointed someone. Specificity is what makes a document survive; it is also what makes
it impossible to have written about anything else.

**Write in one person's voice.** Even for a company project, the sentences should sound
like a person said them. "We decided the user should be able to read the generated code"
is a person. "The system is designed with readability as a core tenet" is a department.

**Prefer the shorter, plainer word.** This document will be read by someone under
pressure, years from now, trying to decide something. Nothing about it should need a
second pass.

**Let it have an edge.** A soul document is allowed to be against something. Projects that
believe nothing in particular produce documents nobody remembers. If the interview turned
up an irritation, keep the irritation.

## Being human without being precious

The opposite failure exists and is nearly as bad: overwritten, self-congratulatory prose
that treats a build tool like a spiritual event. Watch for these:

- Sentence fragments used for drama. Like this. Every other line.
- Grand abstractions the project cannot support — freedom, humanity, the future of work —
  when the project generates type definitions.
- Emotion the interview did not produce. If nobody said they loved it, don't write that.
- Manifesto cadence — long strings of parallel clauses that sound like an oath.

The register to aim for is a good engineer explaining, honestly and without ceremony, why
they spent a year on something. Warm, specific, a little opinionated, not solemn.

## Tone by project type

The soul is the same; the voice around it differs.

| Type | Register |
|---|---|
| **Developer tool / library** | Direct, technical, assumes competence. Trade-offs stated plainly |
| **Game** | Can be playful and can use the game's own voice. The origin story matters most here |
| **Application for non-developers** | Plain language, no jargon, concrete user situations |
| **Community project** | Uses "we" honestly — say who "we" is. Governance and belonging matter more |
| **Startup / commercial** | Must be honest about money. A soul document that pretends revenue isn't a force is the first one to be betrayed |
| **Personal project** | First person singular is fine and usually better. "I built this because" is the most honest sentence in the genre |

## Length

200–400 lines for the whole document. Sections that run long are usually padding —
Origin Story earns five paragraphs, Our Belief does not.

If a section has nothing real in it, leave the marked gap. An honest hole is worth more
than a filled one, and it invites the person to come back and answer.
